# Mystery Jam: Interactive Mystery Brainstorming

## Overview

Help brainstorm and refine mystery game ideas through collaborative dialogue. Apply proven storytelling principles from professional mystery writers to explore concepts, identify plot holes, and develop mysteries that are fair, logical, and satisfying to solve.

**This is a brainstorming tool, not a code generator.** The goal is to help you think through your mystery idea and apply sound mystery-writing principles through conversation.

## Core Mystery Principles

Based on the Mystery Writers of America handbook and interactive game design:

1. **Fair Play**: All clues needed to solve the mystery must be accessible
2. **The Three Questions**: Every mystery must answer Who, How, and Why
3. **Character Depth**: Each suspect needs distinct personality and believable motives
4. **Quality Clues**: Balance between obvious evidence and red herrings
5. **Immersive Setting**: Location and atmosphere that feels authentic
6. **Logical Timeline**: Events occur in believable sequence with no impossibilities
7. **Red Herring Balance**: Misleading clues that make sense but point wrong
8. **Progressive Revelation**: Information unfolds gradually, building complexity
9. **Satisfying Resolution**: Surprising yet inevitable in retrospect
10. **Player Agency**: Allow investigation in player's preferred order

## The Brainstorming Process

### Understanding the Idea

**Start by listening:**
- What's the vague idea or concept?
- What inspired it?
- What parts are exciting?
- What feels unclear or uncertain?

**Then explore through questions (one at a time):**
- What type of crime fits this concept?
- What setting would make this compelling?
- Who might be the culprit and what would drive them?
- What makes this mystery unique or interesting?

### Phase 1: Foundation (The Solution First)

Help develop the solution first, then work backwards to create clues.

**Core questions to explore:**
- What type of crime? (murder, theft, fraud, kidnapping, etc.)
- What's the setting? (time period, location, atmosphere)
- Who is the culprit and why did they do it? (motive is crucial)
- How exactly did they commit the crime? (method must be plausible)
- What's unique or interesting about this mystery?

**Validate the solution through discussion:**
- Is the motive compelling and believable?
- Is the method physically/logically possible?
- Does the culprit have opportunity and means?
- Is there a satisfying emotional core to the story?

### Phase 2: Cast of Characters (3-5 suspects minimum)

Help brainstorm characters through questions. For each character, explore:

- **Name & Role**: Who they are in relation to the crime
- **Personality**: 2-3 distinct traits that affect how they speak
- **Motive**: Why might they have committed the crime? (even innocents need potential motives)
- **Alibi/Opportunity**: Where were they? Does it hold up?
- **Secrets**: What are they hiding? (doesn't have to be the crime)
- **Relationships**: How do they connect to victim/culprit/other suspects?

**The culprit should:**
- Be introduced early (ideally in first third of suspects)
- Have a believable motive that makes emotional sense
- Not be obviously guilty from the start
- Have personality beyond just "the villain"

### Phase 3: Clue Web (Evidence Distribution)

Help design clues through discussion. Aim for 8-12 clues of varying types:

**Physical Evidence:**
- Objects found at the scene
- Forensic details (fingerprints, substances, etc.)
- Documents, messages, records

**Testimonial Evidence:**
- What characters say during interrogation
- Contradictions between testimonies
- Information revealed through conversation

**Timeline Evidence:**
- When things happened
- Who was where when
- Alibis that do/don't hold up

**For each clue, discuss:**
- Type: physical, testimonial, timeline, or deduction
- Point value: How valuable is this clue? (1-10 points)
- What it reveals: What does this tell the player?
- How it connects: Link to characters, timeline, or method
- Red herring or real: Does it point to truth or mislead?

**Clue Distribution Guidelines:**
- At least 3 clues should strongly point to the culprit
- Include 2-3 red herrings that seem significant but aren't
- Every suspect should have at least 2 clues related to them
- Clues should interconnect (one clue makes another make sense)
- Total possible points should be ~80-100

### Phase 4: Timeline Construction

Work together to build a chronological sequence of events:

**Include:**
- Events leading up to the crime (days/hours before)
- The crime itself (exact timeframe)
- Events after the crime (discovery, initial reactions)
- Key moments that establish alibis or opportunity

**Timeline should:**
- Have 5-8 key events
- Include specific times where possible
- Create windows of opportunity for culprit
- Rule out some suspects through alibis
- Include at least one misleading event

### Phase 5: Case File Details

Discuss the crime scene and initial investigation:

**Victim details:**
- Who/what is the victim?
- Why were they targeted?
- Background relevant to motive

**Discovery details:**
- Who found the victim/crime scene?
- When and where?
- Initial observations

**Physical evidence at scene:**
- 3-5 pieces of initial evidence
- Mix of obvious and subtle clues
- Some should require interrogation to understand

### Phase 6: Interrogation Questions

For each character, explore 3-5 interrogation questions:

**Question types:**
- Open-ended ("Tell me about...")
- Direct ("Where were you at...")
- Accusatory ("Why did you...")
- About relationships ("What's your connection to...")

**Responses should:**
- Reveal personality through voice/style
- Provide clues (directly or indirectly)
- Sometimes be evasive or misleading
- Connect to other characters' testimonies

### Phase 7: Validation & Balancing

Review the complete mystery concept:

**Fair Play Check:**
- Can the player logically deduce the culprit from clues alone?
- Are all necessary clues accessible?
- Is the solution satisfying in hindsight?

**Balance Check:**
- Minimum points to accuse: ~30 points (safety threshold)
- Perfect score threshold: ~80 points (found most evidence)
- Multiple investigation paths lead to solution
- Red herrings are believable but solvable

**Difficulty Check:**
- Beginner: Culprit obvious by 60% of clues
- Medium: Requires 70-80% of clues to be confident
- Hard: Need 80%+ clues and careful deduction

## Collaborative Approach

**How to brainstorm:**
- Ask one question at a time
- Use multiple choice when appropriate to make decisions easier
- Let the user explore ideas freely
- Point out potential issues or plot holes
- Suggest alternatives when something doesn't work
- Apply mystery principles naturally in conversation
- Be ready to go back and revise earlier decisions

**Present ideas in sections:**
- Break complex ideas into digestible chunks (200-300 words)
- Check after each section if it's heading in the right direction
- Be flexible - mysteries evolve during brainstorming
- Don't lock in decisions too early

**When ideas get concrete:**
- Summarize what's been decided
- Highlight what still needs work
- Identify any logic gaps or fairness issues
- Suggest next steps for development

## Process Flow

1. **Listen first**: Understand the vague idea and what excites the user
2. **Ask questions**: One at a time, explore the concept
3. **Build solution first**: Define who, how, why before anything else
4. **Design backwards**: Create clues that lead to the solution
5. **One topic at a time**: Discuss each aspect (characters, clues, timeline) thoroughly
6. **Iterate freely**: Go back and adjust based on feedback
7. **Test logic**: Does the timeline work? Are clues fair? Is motive believable?
8. **Summarize**: Recap decisions and highlight remaining work

**Don't automatically generate code or JSON files** - The user will decide when/if they want to formalize their mystery into game format.

## Key Principles

- **Listen first, ask second** - Understand the user's vision before guiding
- **One question at a time** - Don't overwhelm with multiple questions
- **Solution first, clues second** - Know the answer before creating the mystery
- **Explore, don't prescribe** - Help the user discover their story
- **Multiple suspects required** - Need at least 3 viable suspects
- **Fair play mandatory** - Player must be able to solve it logically
- **Personality in dialogue** - Characters should sound distinct
- **Test the logic** - Walk through the player's path to solution
- **Brainstorm, don't build** - Focus on ideas and concepts, not implementation

## After Brainstorming

**Document the concept:**
- Summarize key decisions made
- List unresolved questions
- Note what still needs work
- Highlight any logic issues found

**Next steps (ask the user what they want):**
- Could formalize into JSON files for the game
- Could write a full design document
- Could start implementing specific characters
- Could test the logic more rigorously
- Could just let the ideas sit and marinate

**Don't assume next steps** - Ask what the user wants to do with their mystery idea.

## Example Starting Points

Good mystery jam prompts:
- "I have a vague idea about a murder at a 1920s speakeasy"
- "What if the mystery took place on a space station?"
- "I want to explore a locked-room murder but need help with the method"
- "I have a culprit and motive but not sure how to create suspects"
- "Can you help me figure out if my timeline actually works?"
- "I want to adapt [story/movie] into a mystery game"
- "Help me brainstorm red herrings for my mystery"

The skill will ask questions to explore and refine your idea through conversation, not generate code!
